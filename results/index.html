The evidence

Every number on the slide above, with the measurement behind it.

912 cup_on_saucer episodes · DINOv2-base on a Modal L4 · python run_all.py regenerates all of it in 1-2 minutes, on a laptop, with no GPU and no credentials

The deliverable: a score that ranks two subsets

Diverse 32
1.97

Same budget, same pool, same encoder — the diverse subset scores 1.2× higher. The thumbnails are the score made visible: the random grid repeats near-identical clips, the diverse grid does not. Selection is cluster cover (k-means, then the real episode nearest each centroid); the score is the Vendi Score, read as "effectively N distinct episodes". Cluster cover is chosen over pure farthest-point deliberately: FPS scores far higher (3.31) but collects outliers and represents only 10% of the corpus against cluster cover's 59%. The highest score is not the best subset.

Is the gap luck?

Vendi score 1.93 ± 0.14 (range 1.67–2.36) 1.97 beats only 61% of draws — inside the noise
share of corpus represented 36.9% ± 3.3 (best of 300: 45.4%) 59.2% beats all 300, z = 6.7

This is why we lead with coverage. A single random draw proves nothing until you know the spread behind it, so we measured it: on Vendi alone the two overlap, and no random draw out of 300 came within 14 points of our coverage. Reporting both is what makes the second number mean something.

Does the score rank what a human already knows?

subset of 32Vendi score
one operator, one recording day 1.53 ± 0.13 (highest 1.85) a human would call this narrow
spread across all 10 recording days 2.69 ± 0.08 (lowest 2.56) broader in 50/50 paired trials, zero overlap

No selector is involved here — this tests the score, not our choice of how to pick. Two subsets anyone would already agree differ, and the number agrees, every single trial, with the highest narrow score (1.85) still below the lowest broad one (2.56). That is the track's ask — "a score that ranks two subsets" — with the ranking checkable against something other than our own opinion.

The score tracks things it was never shown

recording sessions covered

At k=16 the diverse subset reaches 5.5 of 10 recording days against random's 4.4 — +24%.

prop combinations covered

12 distinct cup/saucer combinations exist in the registry metadata.

Neither the recording date nor the objects field is visible to the encoder — it only ever saw pixels. So a subset the score calls more diverse covering more sessions and more prop combinations is external evidence, not the score admiring its own geometry.

survives the duplication test yes — -0.047 on exact clones no — 45 → 67 when 30% of the clips are replaced by copies

The judge saw exactly the contact sheets above and was asked for a 0–100 diversity rating, 5 times each, then 5 more at temperature 0. It is doing a harder job — reading semantics with no reference set — and it writes an explanation we cannot. As a ranking instrument, though, it is not reproducible and cannot be falsified. That is the job we replaced.

Why this compounds

The index costs $0.057 once and answers every subsequent question for nothing. The judge charges $0.0016 each time, so it is cheaper right up until the 35th question — and we are honest that in pure dollars this stays small: 10,000 scorings is only $16 of API spend.

Cost is the small argument. The one that decides it is that those 10,000 questions are 4 hours of serial API latency against milliseconds of numpy — and that the LLM's answers are not the same answers twice. An index turns diversity into a property you look up. A judge keeps it a question you re-buy, at a slightly different price and a slightly different answer, every time you ask.

The limits, measured

Exact duplicates cannot raise the score (-0.047) — that is a property of the metric, and it holds here. Near-duplicates are where it ends: jitter a copy by a cosine distance of 0.0094 — below the 0.0424 that separates genuinely distinct episodes — and the score begins to inflate, reaching +0.661 at 0.125. We measured the threshold rather than claim there wasn't one — a score you can state the breaking point of is a measurement, and one you can't is an opinion.

What's next

Frames that matter, not frames spaced by time

We sample 32 frames uniformly in time — a statement about the clock, not about the task. The frames that carry the manipulation are the first contact, the object changing state and the release, and they are not evenly spaced. Selecting those should sharpen every number on this page, because the episode vector would describe the action instead of the average.

We know the cheap route does not get there. Four CPU policies — thumbnail farthest-point, motion peaks, motion-gated — were measured against a full-GPU oracle and none beat np.linspace (0.0% of the gap closed), and keyframe pooling scored below mean pooling (+0.319 vs +0.352). That result is what makes uniform the defensible default today, and it is what points the next round at content-aware selection: contact and action-boundary detection, plus the registry's own segments annotations.

Same harness, same oracle metric — so the next attempt gets adopted or falsified exactly the way this one was.

Method. 32 uniformly spaced frames per episode → DINOv2-base (768-d, self-supervised, no text tower) on a Modal L4 → mean-pooled to one vector per episode → Vendi Score, the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalues of the normalised cosine kernel.
Uniform sampling is a measured choice, not a default: four CPU keyframe-selection policies were tried and all lost to np.linspace, and keyframe pooling lost to mean pooling (+0.319 vs +0.352 separation). Both experiments are in the repo.
