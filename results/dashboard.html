Neither the recording date nor the objects field is visible to the encoder — it only ever saw pixels. So a subset the score calls more diverse covering more sessions and more prop combinations is external evidence, not the score admiring its own geometry.

The judge saw exactly the contact sheets above and was asked for a 0–100 diversity rating, 5 times each, then 5 more at temperature 0. To be fair to it: it is doing a harder job — reading semantics with no reference set — and it writes an explanation we cannot. The narrow claim is that as a ranking instrument it is not reproducible, and it cannot be falsified the way a metric can.

This is the structural difference, not a tuning win: an embedding is a one-off index, a judge is a per-query cost. Curation is not one question — it is thousands of subset comparisons in a loop.

How it could be fooled

Exact duplicates cannot raise the score (-0.047) — that is a property of the metric, and it holds here. Near-duplicates are the honest caveat: jitter a copy by a cosine distance of 0.0094 — below the 0.0424 that separates genuinely distinct episodes — and the score begins to inflate, reaching +0.661 at 0.125. We measured the threshold rather than claim there wasn't one. A second known limit: farthest-point is the wrong tool for the last rare category — it needs ~101 episodes to cover all 12 prop combos where random needs ~60.

Method. 32 uniformly spaced frames per episode → DINOv2-base (768-d, self-supervised, no text tower) on a Modal L4 → mean-pooled to one vector per episode → Vendi Score, the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalues of the normalised cosine kernel.
Uniform sampling is a measured choice, not a default: four CPU keyframe-selection policies were tried and all lost to np.linspace, and keyframe pooling lost to mean pooling (+0.319 vs +0.352 separation). Both experiments are in the repo.
